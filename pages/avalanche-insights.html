A consultancy that read survey responses by hand wanted to become a software company. Before designing anything, I had to find out who would pay for it, and what they were afraid of.

  • $20kMonthly recurring revenue from the first SaaS customers
  • 6 moTo launch the beta platform
  • SeedRound led by a top VC, pitched on the prototypes
  • 14%Of HR respondents already used automated text tools

The briefService to SaaS

Avalanche Insights pulled themes out of open-ended text for progressive political clients. It worked, but growth depended on analyst time.

The goal was a product that let a general user upload their own survey data, then explore the themes and insights without us in the room. There was no existing product and no users yet.

Goals
  • A platform that surfaces themes and insights
  • Intuitive enough for a non-expert to explore alone
Tools
  • Lucid, Respondent, Zoom
  • Miro, Confluence, Figma

Who is it for?Survey & competitor research

We ran LinkedIn ads offering a way to analyse open-ended text, then surveyed everyone who responded. Four groups came back: HR, customer experience, brand managers and market researchers.

HR managers were the largest group, and 90% more likely than anyone else to analyse feedback by reading every response manually. Almost none of them used automation, yet they were the most enthusiastic about a tool that combined AI with human judgement.

What they fearedConcerns became requirements

Enthusiasm came with conditions. HR managers were handling sensitive employee data and worried about ethics, privacy and whether AI output could be trusted. Surfacing that this early meant each concern could shape the product.

What users worried aboutWhat we built
Can I trust what the AI tells me?Explore responses. Every theme opens onto the raw answers behind it.
Keeping a human in the analysisHuman-in-the-loop themes. AI gives a head start; the user edits everything.
Privacy of employee dataAdmin controls. Simple roles and permissions over who sees what.
Fitting existing tools and processesUpload from anywhere. Exports from any survey platform, with little engineering.

The journeyWhere it hurts

  1. Excited

    Collect

    Fielding the survey. Existing tools handle this well.

  2. Overwhelmed

    Analyse

    Sifting every response for themes. Survey tools offer features here, but none good enough.

  3. Challenged

    Curate

    Pulling charts, quotes and highlights into a story for the organisation.

  4. Relieved

    Share

    Handing over findings and recommendations.

Shaping itStructure, sketches, tests

Analyse became the product. I mapped the sitemap with product and engineering, then moved from sketches to interactive prototypes, testing each step with the people we had interviewed.

To move fast with a tiny team, I built on an open-source design system. It kept pages consistent, sped up handoff and left my time for the problems nobody had solved yet.

The productKey flows in beta

  1. Upload

    Accepts exports from any survey platform, smoothing their differences without heavy engineering.

  2. Start analysis

    Smart themes give users a head start instead of a blank page. How this flow was redesigned.

  3. Dashboard

    The consultancy's static report charts, made interactive for self-serve exploration.

  4. Group and filter

    Segment by demographic in one toggle, or combine attributes into custom segments.

  5. Explore responses

    Click any bar to read the answers behind it in a drawer, without losing your place.

  6. Share

    Tailored report pages for each stakeholder, sent by invite or email.

OutcomeAnd what I took from it

The prototypes carried the investor pitch to a seed round led by a top VC. The beta launched within six months, and the first SaaS customers, including a global streaming platform, brought in $20k a month.

The proprietary data science wasn't fully productised yet, so parts of the beta ran as a "magic box", with manual work behind the scenes while the models caught up.

Lesson

On a blank canvas, research is the design. Personas and the journey map were the reference point for every decision, and they changed with every round of interviews.
